With your own domain, you reach your services under names like `fotos.__DOMAIN__` instead of an IP address and port. You also need it for valid TLS certificates from Let's Encrypt. The article [DNS & domains](/en/wiki/dns-domains/) explains how DNS works behind the scenes.

<OneWay />

<Steps>

<Step title="Check your connection">
Before you spend money: check whether your server can be reachable from outside at all. Many connections (especially cable and fiber) no longer have their own public IPv4 address, but DS-Lite or CGNAT instead. Then you share an IPv4 with other customers, and incoming connections don't reach you.

Ask for your public address on the server:

```bash
curl -4 ifconfig.me
curl -6 ifconfig.me
```

Compare the IPv4 with the WAN or internet address your router shows in its interface. If both are the same, you have your own public IPv4. If the router shows an address from `100.64.0.0/10` (that is `100.64.x.x` to `100.127.x.x`) or no IPv4 at all, you're behind CGNAT or DS-Lite.

In that case, the A records below won't help you much. Read [CGNAT & DS-Lite](/en/wiki/cgnat-ds-lite/) and use a [Cloudflare Tunnel](/en/setup/cloudflare-tunnel/) or a VPN like Tailscale for access. You still need a domain.
</Step>

<Step title="Buy a domain">
You buy a domain from a registrar, a company that manages and sells domains. For common endings like `.de` or `.com`, you usually pay a few euros up to about 20 euros a year.

What to look out for:

- **The renewal price**, not just the first-year price. Teaser offers often get much more expensive afterwards.
- **DNS management with an API or DynDNS**, in case your IP changes (see below).
- **A short, neutral name.** It shows up in every address. Your real name isn't needed.

If you want to use a [Cloudflare Tunnel](/en/setup/cloudflare-tunnel/) later, Cloudflare has to take over DNS management. That also works with a domain from another registrar: you simply enter Cloudflare's name servers there.
</Step>

<Step title="Create A and AAAA records">
A DNS record maps a name to an address. An **A record** points to an IPv4 address, an **AAAA record** to an IPv6 address.

Open your provider's DNS management and create these records:

| Type | Name | Value | TTL |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | `@` (the domain itself) | your public IPv4 | 300 |
| A | `*` | your public IPv4 | 300 |

The `*` record is a **wildcard record**: every subdomain like `fotos.__DOMAIN__` or `passwort.__DOMAIN__` then points to your server. So you don't have to create a record for every new service. Which service answers is decided later by the reverse proxy, based on the name.

The **TTL** (time to live) says how many seconds other servers may cache a record. 300 seconds is a good value while you're still trying things out.

For IPv6, create the same two records as AAAA records. Careful: IPv6 has no NAT. The value is the IPv6 address **of the server**, not of the router:

```bash
ip -6 addr show scope global
```

Use the address that isn't marked `temporary`. For IPv6, you also have to open the ports on the router for exactly this device. If you're unsure, leave the AAAA record out for now. A wrong AAAA record is worse than none, because many devices prefer IPv6.
</Step>

<Step title="Set up DynDNS if your IP changes">
On most home connections, the public IP changes now and then, for example after a router restart. Then your A record points nowhere. **DynDNS** (dynamic DNS) solves this: a small program reports every new IP to your DNS provider.

The easiest way is the router. The FRITZ!Box, for example, has a form for an update URL, user name and password under "Internet → Permit Access → DynDNS". Your DNS provider's help tells you which values to enter. Many registrars offer separate DynDNS credentials for this.

If your router can't do it, or your provider only supports an API, a DynDNS client runs on the server. A common one is [ddclient](https://ddclient.net/), which is also available as a container. It supports many providers, including Cloudflare.

Use DynDNS to update the `@` record. Your wildcard record `*` can then be a **CNAME** to `__DOMAIN__` instead of an A record. A CNAME is a reference to another name, so you only have to keep one record up to date.

If you have a static IP, for example on a rented server, skip this step.
</Step>

<Step title="Check the records">
`dig` queries DNS records directly. On Debian and Ubuntu it's in the `bind9-dnsutils` package:

```bash
sudo apt install bind9-dnsutils
dig +short __DOMAIN__ A
dig +short test.__DOMAIN__ A
dig +short __DOMAIN__ AAAA
```

All queries should print your IP addresses. `test` stands for any subdomain; it's answered by the wildcard record. If nothing comes back, ask a public resolver directly. That bypasses your router's cache:

```bash
dig +short test.__DOMAIN__ A @1.1.1.1
```

On Windows and macOS, `nslookup` works too:

```bash
nslookup test.__DOMAIN__
```

New records are often visible after a few minutes. If you've only just changed the name servers, it can take a few hours. As long as DNS isn't right, the reverse proxy won't get a certificate later; see [Certificate missing](/en/wiki/certificate/).
</Step>

<Step title="Forward ports 80 and 443">
For requests from the internet to reach your server, the router forwards ports 80 (HTTP) and 443 (HTTPS) to your server's static IP. The reverse proxy needs these two ports, and Let's Encrypt uses them to check that you own the domain. How that works and what it means for security is covered in [Port forwarding](/en/wiki/port-forwarding/).

Optionally, add 443 over UDP so browsers can use HTTP/3. Don't forward any other ports, especially not ports of individual services. The reverse proxy handles everything else. If you still don't get a certificate later, [Certificate missing](/en/wiki/certificate/) helps you find the cause.

Next, continue with the [reverse proxy with Traefik](/en/setup/reverse-proxy-traefik/) or the simpler [Nginx Proxy Manager](/en/setup/reverse-proxy-npm/).
</Step>

</Steps>
