You don't need a server from a data center to get started. Most of the services on this site run smoothly on a small, energy-efficient computer. Low power consumption, enough RAM, and an SSD are more important than high performance. This guide will help you make a decision in just a few steps. You can find more background information in the [hardware overview](/en/wiki/hardware-overview/) and under [“Costs & Power Consumption](/en/wiki/costs-power/).”

<OneWay />

<Steps>

<Step title="Determine what should be done">
Write down which services you want to run in the first few months. That will determine the hardware more than anything else.

- **Lightweight services** such as Vaultwarden, Uptime Kuma, Pocket ID, or a reverse proxy each require only a small amount of RAM and hardly any CPU resources.
- **Moderately demanding services** like Nextcloud or Forgejo require a database and a bit more RAM.
- **Resource-intensive services** like Immich (photos with facial recognition) or media servers with transcoding (real-time video conversion) require more RAM, a fast CPU, and often an integrated graphics processor.

Also, think about how much data you want to store. A few passwords take up very little space, but a photo collection can easily amount to several hundred gigabytes.
</Step>

<Step title="Choose between on-premises or in the cloud">
Before you buy hardware, it’s worth asking yourself whether you really need to have the server on your premises. A rented virtual private server (VPS) has a fixed public IP address and doesn’t take up space in your living room, but it comes with a monthly fee and usually offers limited storage space. Large amounts of data, such as photos, are better stored at home.

If you're unsure, the article "[Cloud, Hybrid, or On-Premises?](/en/wiki/cloud-hybrid-local/)" offers a brief decision-making guide.
</Step>

<Step title="Select Device Type">
For a home lab, these are the main devices to consider:

| Device | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mini PC** | Energy-efficient, quiet, compact, x86-compatible, with SSD and RAM that are often upgradeable | Limited space for additional hard drives |
| **Old Laptop** | It's free, the battery bridges short power outages, and it has a built-in screen and keyboard | Often just one drive; the battery degrades with continuous use; Wi-Fi instead of Ethernet on some models |
| **NAS** (Synology, QNAP, etc.) | Plenty of space for hard drives, RAID, and a finished surface | Custom operating system, limited Docker support, often a weak CPU and little RAM |
| **DIY / Old Desktop PC** | Plenty of power and space for drives | Usually uses significantly more electricity, is larger, and is louder |
| **VPS** | Fixed IP, no need for your own device, quick setup | Ongoing costs, limited storage, data stored with the provider |

For most beginners, a **used or new mini-PC** is the best compromise. Devices with the latest energy-efficient Intel processors (such as the N series) or comparable AMD chips are sufficient for running multiple applications at the same time. An old laptop is ideal for trying it out for free at first.

<Callout title="And what about the Raspberry Pi?">
A Raspberry Pi works, but it has an ARM CPU. Not every Docker image is available for ARM, and SD cards tend to fail when used continuously. If you're using a Pi, go with a newer model that has an SSD instead of an SD card. The guides on this page are based on an x86 computer.
</Callout>
</Step>

<Step title="Set RAM">
With Docker, RAM is usually the first bottleneck—long before the CPU. Every container needs its own share, especially databases.

- **8 GB**: a good starting point for a handful of light-to-moderate apps.
- **16 GB**: plenty of space, even with Nextcloud or Immich, and a little to spare.
- **32 GB and more**: useful if you want to run additional virtual machines, for example with [Proxmox VE](/en/wiki/proxmox-ve/).

When buying, check to see if the RAM can be upgraded later. On some mini PCs, it's soldered in place.
</Step>

<Step title="Plan Storage">
The system and your containers should be stored on an **SSD**. It's fast, quiet, and energy-efficient. An SSD with 500 GB to 1 TB is enough for the system, containers, and plenty of application data.

For large amounts of data, such as photos, videos, or backups, you can also plan to add a larger hard drive (HDD) or a second SSD. Check how many drives your device can accommodate: Many mini PCs have an M.2 slot and sometimes an additional 2.5-inch bay.

<Callout type="warning" title="Two hard drives are no substitute for a backup">
A RAID array or a second hard drive protects against hard drive failure, but not against accidental deletion, ransomware, or water damage. You'll set up backups later in the guide "[Backups with borgmatic](/en/setup/backups-borgmatic/)."
</Callout>
</Step>

<Step title="Estimate Electricity Consumption">
Your server runs 24/7. That's why idle power consumption is what matters most, since a home lab spends most of its time in this state.

Rule of thumb: **Watts × 8.76 = kWh per year**. So, a device with a continuous power consumption of 10 W uses about 88 kWh per year. Multiply that by your electricity rate per kWh, and you'll know the annual cost.

Approximate values at idle:

| Device | Idle (approximately) |
| --- | --- |
| Energy-Efficient Mini PC | 5–15 W |
| Laptop with the lid closed | 5–15 W |
| NAS with two hard drives | 15–30 W |
| Older desktop PC | 30–60 W and more |

The actual values depend heavily on the model, the drives, and the BIOS settings. The most accurate way to measure them is with a power meter that plugs into an outlet. For more information, see "[Costs & Power Consumption](/en/wiki/costs-power/)."
</Step>

<Step title="Check Before Buying">
Go through this short list before you buy or use a device:

- Is there **an Ethernet port**? A server should be connected to the network via a cable, not via Wi-Fi.
- **x86 CPU** (Intel or AMD) so that all images will run?
- **At least 8 GB of RAM**—is it better if it's upgradeable?
- **SSD** as the system drive?
- Can the BIOS **automatically turn back on** after a power outage? Depending on the manufacturer, this option may be called “Restore on AC Power Loss” or “Power On after Power Failure,” for example.
- Is the device **quiet** enough for the location where it will be placed?

If everything looks good, we'll move on to [installing the operating system](/en/setup/installing-the-os/).
</Step>

</Steps>
