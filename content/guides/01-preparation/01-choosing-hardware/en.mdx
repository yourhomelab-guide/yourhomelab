To get started, you don't need a data center server. Most services on this site run fine on a small, frugal computer. Low power consumption, enough RAM and an SSD matter more than lots of performance. This guide leads you to a decision in a few steps. You'll find the background in the [hardware overview](/en/wiki/hardware-overview/) and under [Costs & power consumption](/en/wiki/costs-power/). If you don't know where to start at all, the [getting started assistant](/en/tools/getting-started/) helps you with a few questions.

<OneWay />

<Steps>

<Step title="Decide what should run">
Write down which services you want to run in the first few months. That determines the hardware more than anything else.

- **Light services** like Vaultwarden, Uptime Kuma, Pocket ID or a reverse proxy each need only a little RAM and hardly any CPU.
- **Medium services** like Nextcloud or Forgejo need a database and somewhat more RAM.
- **Heavy services** like Immich (photos with face recognition) or media servers with transcoding (converting videos in real time) want more RAM, a fast CPU and often an integrated graphics unit.

Also think about how much data you want to store. A few passwords are tiny; a photo collection quickly reaches several hundred gigabytes.
</Step>

<Step title="Decide: at home or in the cloud">
Before you buy hardware, it's worth asking whether the server should be at your place at all. A rented virtual server (VPS) has a fixed public IP and no device in your living room, but costs a monthly fee and usually offers little storage. Large amounts of data like photos are better off at home.

If you're unsure, the article [Cloud, hybrid or local?](/en/wiki/cloud-hybrid-local/) helps with a short decision guide.
</Step>

<Step title="Choose the type of device">
For a homelab at home, these devices are the main candidates:

| Device | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mini PC** | Frugal, quiet, small, x86-compatible, SSD and RAM often upgradable | Little room for additional drives |
| **Old laptop** | Costs nothing, battery bridges short power cuts, screen and keyboard built in | Often only one drive, battery ages in continuous use, Wi-Fi instead of LAN on some models |
| **NAS** (Synology, QNAP and others) | Lots of room for drives, RAID, ready-made interface | Its own operating system, Docker only with limits, often a weak CPU and little RAM |
| **Self-built / old desktop PC** | Lots of performance and room for drives | Usually uses much more power, bigger and louder |
| **VPS** | Fixed IP, no device of your own, quick to set up | Running costs, little storage, data at the provider |

For most beginners, a **used or new mini PC** is the best compromise. Devices with current frugal Intel processors (such as the N series) or comparable AMD chips are enough for many services at the same time. An old laptop is ideal for trying things out for free first.

<Callout title="And the Raspberry Pi?">
A Raspberry Pi works, but has an ARM CPU. Not every Docker image is available for ARM, and SD cards tend to break in continuous use. If you use a Pi, use a current one with an SSD instead of an SD card. The guides on this site assume an x86 computer.

If you have both, use the mini PC as the main server and the Pi for small jobs like DNS. [Multiple devices: splitting the roles](/en/wiki/multiple-devices/) explains how to split things across several devices.
</Callout>
</Step>

<Step title="Decide on RAM">
With Docker, memory (RAM) is usually the first bottleneck, long before the CPU. Every container needs its own share, databases especially.

- **8 GB**: good for starting with a handful of light to medium services.
- **16 GB**: comfortable, even with Nextcloud or Immich and some headroom.
- **32 GB and more**: makes sense if you also want to run virtual machines, for example with [Proxmox VE](/en/wiki/proxmox-ve/). [Operating models at a glance](/en/wiki/operating-models/) shows which combination of system and virtualization suits you.

When buying, check whether the RAM can be upgraded later. On some mini PCs, it's soldered on.
</Step>

<Step title="Plan the storage">
The system and your containers belong on an **SSD**. It's fast, quiet and frugal. An SSD with 500 GB to 1 TB is enough for the system, containers and lots of application data.

For large amounts of data like photos, videos or backups, you can also plan a bigger hard disk (HDD) or a second SSD. Check how many drives your device can take: many mini PCs have an M.2 slot and sometimes an additional 2.5-inch bay.

<Callout type="warning" title="Two disks don't replace a backup">
A RAID or a second disk protects against a disk failing, not against accidental deletion, ransomware or water damage. You'll set up backups later in the guide [Backups with borgmatic](/en/setup/backups-borgmatic/).
</Callout>
</Step>

<Step title="Estimate the power consumption">
Your server runs around the clock. That's why idle consumption matters most, because that's where a homelab spends most of its time.

Rule of thumb: **watts × 8.76 = kWh per year**. A device with a constant 10 W uses about 88 kWh a year. Multiply that by your electricity price per kWh, and you know the yearly cost.

Rough idle figures:

| Device | Idle (approx.) |
| --- | --- |
| Frugal mini PC | 5–15 W |
| Laptop with the lid closed | 5–15 W |
| NAS with two hard disks | 15–30 W |
| Older desktop PC | 30–60 W and more |

The actual values depend heavily on the model, the drives and the BIOS settings. A plug-in power meter measures most accurately. More under [Costs & power consumption](/en/wiki/costs-power/). The [power calculator](/en/tools/power-calculator/) works out the cost for your device and your electricity price.
</Step>

<Step title="Check before buying">
Go through this short list before you buy or use a device:

- **LAN port** available? A server belongs on the network by cable, not on Wi-Fi.
- **x86 CPU** (Intel or AMD), so all images run?
- **At least 8 GB of RAM**, ideally upgradable?
- **SSD** as the system drive?
- Can the BIOS **power on automatically** after a power cut? Depending on the manufacturer, the option is called something like "Restore on AC Power Loss" or "Power On after Power Failure".
- Is the device **quiet** enough for where it will stand?

If everything fits, continue with [Installing the OS](/en/setup/installing-the-os/).
</Step>

</Steps>
