## Why your own domain?

Nobody can remember IP addresses, and on many home connections they change too. A domain solves both: you type `fotos.__DOMAIN__` instead of `192.168.1.20:2283`. You also need a domain to get valid certificates from Let's Encrypt (see [TLS & certificates](/en/wiki/tls-certificates/)).

You rent a domain from a registrar, usually for a small amount per year. Prices depend heavily on the ending. Watch the renewal price, not just the first-year offer, and whether the provider has an API for DNS records. You'll need it later for DNS-01 certificates and DynDNS.

## How DNS works

The **Domain Name System** is the phone book of the internet. When you open an address, your device asks a DNS resolver, usually your router or your provider's. The resolver works its way through to your domain's responsible name server and returns the answer. The responsible one is the name server you entered at the registrar, often the registrar's own.

The answers are stored in **DNS records**. You maintain them in your DNS provider's web interface.

## The most important record types

| Type | Points to | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | an IPv4 address | `__DOMAIN__` → `203.0.113.10` |
| **AAAA** | an IPv6 address | `__DOMAIN__` → `2001:db8::10` |
| **CNAME** | another name | `www.__DOMAIN__` → `__DOMAIN__` |
| **TXT** | any text | Proofs for certificates, mail settings |

A few notes on these:

- **A and AAAA** are the actual signposts to your server. If you have a public IPv6, an AAAA record in addition to the A record is worth it.
- **CNAME** is an alias. It saves work: if the IP changes, you only adjust the one A record. According to the standard, the domain itself (without a subdomain) must not be a CNAME.
- **TXT** rarely shows up in the browser, but often in the background. For the DNS-01 challenge, for example, Let's Encrypt checks a TXT record under `_acme-challenge.__DOMAIN__`.

The addresses `203.0.113.10` and `2001:db8::10` are pure example addresses. Put your own public IP there.

## Wildcard records

A **wildcard record** `*.__DOMAIN__` applies to every subdomain that isn't entered separately. Together with a [reverse proxy](/en/wiki/reverse-proxy-explained/), that's handy: you create `*.__DOMAIN__` as an A record pointing to your IP once. For every new service, you then only configure the proxy, not DNS.

A wildcard record only applies one level deep. `app.__DOMAIN__` matches, `test.app.__DOMAIN__` doesn't.

## TTL: how long answers are valid

Every record has a **TTL** (time to live) in seconds. Resolvers may cache the answer for that long. A TTL of 3600 means: a change can take up to an hour to arrive everywhere.

For records that change often, for example with a changing IP, a short TTL of a few minutes makes sense. If you're planning a move, lower the TTL a few hours beforehand.

You check whether a record is right on the command line. On Debian and Ubuntu, `dig` is in the `bind9-dnsutils` package:

```bash
dig +short A __DOMAIN__
dig +short AAAA __DOMAIN__
```

## DynDNS with a changing IP

Most home connections get a new public IPv4 regularly. **DynDNS** (dynamic DNS) still keeps the A record up to date: a small program notices that the IP has changed and writes the new one into the record through the DNS provider's API.

Often the router already does this; many have a DynDNS feature built in. Alternatively, a small container on the server checks regularly. Important: DynDNS only helps if you have your own public IPv4 at all. With CGNAT or DS-Lite, you don't (see [CGNAT & DS-Lite](/en/wiki/cgnat-ds-lite/)).

## Local DNS and split DNS

On your home network, too, you can use names instead of IPs. A **local DNS server** like Pi-hole, [AdGuard Home](/en/setup/adguard-home/) or the router itself can resolve names the internet doesn't know.

**Split DNS** means: the same name gives a different answer depending on where you are. From outside, `cloud.__DOMAIN__` points to your public IP; on the home network, directly to `192.168.1.20`. Advantages:

- Traffic stays on the home network and doesn't go out through the router and back in again.
- Services that should only be reachable internally still get nice names and valid certificates.

Some routers can't handle requests from inside to their own public IP (missing "NAT loopback" or "hairpinning"). Split DNS helps there too.

<Callout title="Split DNS is optional">
To start with, one record at your DNS provider is enough. You can add local DNS later when you need it.
</Callout>

## In short

- A domain makes your services memorable and is a requirement for Let's Encrypt certificates.
- **A**/**AAAA** point to IPs, **CNAME** to other names, **TXT** carries text like certificate proofs.
- A wildcard record plus a reverse proxy saves you DNS work for every new service.
- If your IP changes, **DynDNS** keeps the record up to date.
