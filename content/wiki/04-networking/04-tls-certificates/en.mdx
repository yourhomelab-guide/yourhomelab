## Why HTTPS at home too

Without HTTPS, passwords, cookies and content travel through the network in plain text. Anyone who can listen in sees them: on public Wi-Fi, at the provider, sometimes even on your own network. On top of that, many services and apps now require HTTPS, such as password managers like Vaultwarden or sign-in with passkeys.

**TLS** (Transport Layer Security) is the technology behind HTTPS. It does two jobs:

- **Encryption**: nobody in between can read or change anything.
- **Authenticity**: the certificate proves that the server really belongs to `vault.__DOMAIN__` and isn't an attacker.

## What a certificate is

A certificate is a digital ID card for one or more names. It's issued by a **certificate authority** (CA). Browsers and operating systems ship a list of CAs they trust. If your certificate is signed by one of these CAs, the browser shows the lock without a warning.

A **self-signed certificate** is one you issue yourself. It encrypts just as well, but no browser trusts it. You get a warning that you have to click away every time or turn off on each device individually. For a homelab with its own domain, there's no reason for that anymore.

## Let's Encrypt and ACME

[Let's Encrypt](https://letsencrypt.org/) is a non-profit CA that issues certificates for free. It all runs fully automatically through the **ACME** protocol. Traefik, Caddy and Nginx Proxy Manager speak ACME out of the box. At most, you enter an email address for your account; the proxy does the rest. Since 2025, Let's Encrypt no longer sends reminder emails before expiry, so automatic renewal has to work.

Before Let's Encrypt issues a certificate, you have to prove that you own the name. That's called a **challenge**. There are three kinds.

## The three challenges

| Challenge | How it's checked | Requirement | Wildcard |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **HTTP-01** | Let's Encrypt fetches a file over port 80 | Port 80 reachable from outside | No |
| **TLS-ALPN-01** | Let's Encrypt opens a special TLS connection on port 443 | Port 443 reachable from outside | No |
| **DNS-01** | Let's Encrypt checks a TXT record `_acme-challenge` in your DNS | API access to your DNS provider | Yes |

**HTTP-01** is the classic and works almost everywhere port 80 is open.

**TLS-ALPN-01** gets by with port 443 alone. This site uses it in the Traefik template (`tlschallenge=true` on the `le` resolver), because 443 is open anyway.

**DNS-01** needs no open port at all. The proxy briefly creates a TXT record through your DNS provider's API, Let's Encrypt checks it, and then it's deleted again. That has two big advantages:

- You get **wildcard certificates** like `*.__DOMAIN__`. One certificate then covers all subdomains.
- It also works for services that are **only** reachable **on your home network or through a VPN**. The server doesn't have to be reachable for Let's Encrypt, only your DNS provider.

The price: you need a DNS provider with an API and store an API token with the proxy. Only give this token the rights it needs, that is editing DNS records of this one zone. Which providers are supported is in your proxy's docs, for example [Traefik](https://doc.traefik.io/traefik/https/acme/#providers) or the [Caddy DNS modules](https://caddyserver.com/docs/modules/).

<Callout title="Which challenge for me?">
If your server is reachable from outside on port 443, TLS-ALPN-01 as in the template is enough. If you have no public IPv4, only want to access things internally or through a VPN, or want a wildcard certificate, use DNS-01.
</Callout>

## Lifetime and renewal

Let's Encrypt certificates are currently valid for 90 days. The lifetime is going down step by step: to 64 days from February 2027 and to 45 days from February 2028 ([announcement](https://letsencrypt.org/2025/12/02/from-90-to-45)). This applies to all public certificate authorities, not just Let's Encrypt. The short lifetime is intentional: a stolen certificate is only usable for a short time, and automation becomes a must.

For you, this means: don't renew anything by hand. Your proxy checks regularly and renews in good time before expiry. All that matters is that it stores its certificates permanently. With Traefik, that's the `acme.json` file under `__DATA(traefik)__/letsencrypt/`. If it gets lost, Traefik fetches new certificates on startup. That works as long as you don't request too often: Let's Encrypt has [rate limits](https://letsencrypt.org/docs/rate-limits/), which can kick in after many restarts with empty storage. If the browser shows a warning instead of a valid certificate, [Certificate missing](/en/wiki/certificate/) helps you find the cause.

<Callout title="For testing: staging">
If you're trying out a lot, use Let's Encrypt's staging environment first. Its certificates aren't trusted, but the rate limits are much more generous. Your proxy's docs explain how to turn it on.
</Callout>

## Certificate Transparency: your subdomains are public

Every publicly trusted certificate ends up in **Certificate Transparency logs**. These are public, searchable directories of all issued certificates. They're meant to prevent a CA from secretly issuing false certificates.

The flip side: anyone can look up which names you use there. If you get a certificate for `nextcloud.__DOMAIN__`, the world knows shortly afterwards that this subdomain exists. Automated scanners evaluate these logs and often try new names just minutes later.

What follows from this:

- An unknown subdomain name is **no protection**. Secure services with a login, updates and, where needed, a login in front like Tinyauth or Pocket ID (see [Keeping the attack surface small](/en/wiki/attack-surface/)).
- With a **wildcard certificate** via DNS-01, only `*.__DOMAIN__` shows up in the log, not every single name.

## In short

- HTTPS encrypts and proves the server's authenticity. For that, every name needs a certificate.
- Let's Encrypt issues it for free; your reverse proxy fetches and renews it automatically.
- HTTP-01 needs port 80, TLS-ALPN-01 port 443, DNS-01 only a DNS provider with an API, and it can do wildcards.
- All certificates are publicly visible, and so are your subdomains.
