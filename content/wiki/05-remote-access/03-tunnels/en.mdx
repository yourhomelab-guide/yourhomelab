## Why a tunnel

[Port forwarding](/en/wiki/port-forwarding/) only works if requests from the internet reach your router at all. On many connections, they don't: with [CGNAT or DS-Lite](/en/wiki/cgnat-ds-lite/), you share the IPv4 address with your provider's other customers. Nobody gets through to you from outside.

A **tunnel** reverses the direction. It's not the internet that connects to your server, but your server that connects to a computer on the internet. Outgoing connections are always allowed. Through this standing connection, the computer outside sends your visitors' requests to you at home.

This is how it works:

1. A small program runs on your server, the tunnel client.
2. It opens an encrypted connection to a server on the internet and keeps it open.
3. Your domain points to this server, not to your connection.
4. When someone opens your domain, the request lands there and is passed through the tunnel to your reverse proxy or directly to a container.

Your home IP address doesn't show up anywhere, and no port is open on the router.

## Cloudflare Tunnel

[Cloudflare Tunnel](https://developers.cloudflare.com/cloudflare-one/networks/connectors/cloudflare-tunnel/) is the best-known service of this kind. The `cloudflared` program runs on your server, usually as a container. It connects to Cloudflare's network. In the Cloudflare dashboard, you define which host name belongs to which service.

Advantages:

- Free for personal use.
- No port forwarding; works behind CGNAT.
- Quick to set up; certificates come from Cloudflare.
- With Cloudflare Access, you can put a login in front of individual services.

What you should know:

- **Your domain has to be at Cloudflare.** More precisely: Cloudflare has to manage the DNS records. For that, you switch your domain's name servers to Cloudflare.
- **Cloudflare sees your traffic.** TLS encryption ends at Cloudflare. There, the traffic is unencrypted before it goes through the tunnel to you. For a blog, that doesn't matter; for a password manager, it's worth thinking about.
- **Large files and streaming:** Cloudflare limits the size of individual uploads, and the terms of service restrict serving large amounts of video through the free offering. For uploading large files to Nextcloud or media streaming, the tunnel is therefore often not the right way. Check the current terms before you rely on it.
- **Dependency:** if Cloudflare goes down or changes the rules, your services aren't reachable from outside.

[Cloudflare Tunnel](/en/setup/cloudflare-tunnel/) shows how to set up the tunnel.

## Your own VPS as the entrance

If you don't want a big provider in between, rent a small **VPS** (virtual server) with a public IP and build the tunnel yourself. The VPS is then your public entrance, and the data stays at home.

The basic idea is always the same: your home server connects to the VPS via **WireGuard**. On the VPS, a reverse proxy receives the requests on 80 and 443 and forwards them through the WireGuard tunnel to your home server. Since the connection starts from home, this also works behind CGNAT.

You can build this by hand or use a ready-made project. [Pangolin](https://github.com/fosrl/pangolin) is one such project: you install it on the VPS, a small client runs on the home server, and Pangolin takes care of the WireGuard connection, reverse proxy, certificates and, if you like, a login in front of your services.

Compared to Cloudflare:

- **More control:** TLS can end at your place, and there are no size or streaming rules from a tunnel provider.
- **More work:** you run and update a second server that sits directly on the internet.
- **Running costs:** a small VPS costs a monthly fee, usually a few euros.
- **Bandwidth:** everything runs through the VPS and your connection's upload at home.

<Callout type="warning" title="A tunnel isn't login protection">
A tunnel hides your home IP, but makes your services just as public as port forwarding. Every login page at the end of the tunnel can be attacked from anywhere. Admin interfaces don't belong outside here either, and you protect public services additionally, for example with [Tinyauth](/en/setup/tinyauth/) or [Pocket ID](/en/setup/pocket-id/).
</Callout>

## Tunnel or VPN?

Both build an encrypted connection, but the purpose differs:

- A **tunnel** makes services reachable for **everyone**, in the browser, without software on the visitor's side.
- A [VPN](/en/wiki/vpn/) makes your home network reachable only for **you**, with an app on your device.

Many homelabs combine both: public services through a tunnel or port forwarding, everything administrative only through a VPN. [Access methods compared](/en/wiki/access-methods-compared/) gives an overview.
