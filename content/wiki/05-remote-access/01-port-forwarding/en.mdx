## Why you need port forwarding

Your router separates your home network from the internet. From inside to outside, everything gets through; from outside to inside, nothing does at first. When a request arrives from the internet, the router doesn't know which device is meant and drops it.

**Port forwarding** changes that for a specific port. You tell the router: "Send everything that arrives on port 443 to my server `__SERVER_IP__`." That makes your server reachable on this port for the whole world. Not just for you, but also for every scanner that searches the internet for open ports.

A **port** is a number through which a computer can offer several services at the same time. More in [IP, ports & NAT](/en/wiki/ip-ports-nat/).

## Only 80 and 443, and only to the reverse proxy

The most important rule: forward exactly two ports, **80 (HTTP) and 443 (HTTPS)**, and send both to your [reverse proxy](/en/wiki/reverse-proxy-explained/). The proxy receives all requests, checks the host name and passes them on to the right container. It takes care of the TLS certificates and can put login protection like Tinyauth or Pocket ID in front.

That way you have exactly one entrance to keep an eye on. Every further service only gets a subdomain, not an open port of its own.

You mainly need port 80 for redirecting HTTP to HTTPS and, depending on the setup, for Let's Encrypt's certificate check. If your proxy gets certificates through the DNS challenge and you can do without the redirect, 443 alone is enough.

<Callout type="warning" title="Never forward directly">
Admin interfaces (Portainer, router, NAS or Proxmox web interfaces), SSH (port 22), databases (such as 3306 or 5432) and dashboards never belong directly on the internet. These services are targeted specifically, and a single weak password or an old version is enough. For that kind of access on the go, use a [VPN](/en/wiki/vpn/).
</Callout>

## How to set it up

The menus are named differently on every router, but the process is always similar:

1. **Static IP for the server:** the router has to know where to forward to. Give your server a fixed address on your home network (see [Static IP & hostname](/en/setup/static-ip-hostname/)).
2. **Create the forwarding:** in the router, select the server and forward ports 80 and 443 (TCP). On a FRITZ!Box, you'll find this under "Internet → Permit Access".
3. **Firewall on the server:** 80 and 443 must be open there too, with UFW for example (see [Firewall with UFW](/en/setup/firewall-ufw/)).
4. **Test from outside:** open your domain over the mobile network, not from your Wi-Fi. From your own network, calling the public address doesn't work with some routers, even though everything works from outside. If the browser warns about an invalid certificate, [Certificate missing](/en/wiki/certificate/) helps.

So your domain always points to the right address, you also need **DynDNS** if your IPv4 address changes. The router or a small program then reports the new address to your DNS provider automatically. More in [DNS & domains](/en/wiki/dns-domains/).

<Callout title="Docker and UFW">
Ports you publish with `ports:` in a `compose.yaml` are opened by Docker directly in the firewall, bypassing UFW. So only publish the reverse proxy's ports. All other services sit in the proxy network `__NETWORK__` without `ports:`.
</Callout>

## IPv6: forwarding means a firewall rule here

With IPv4, all devices on your home network share one public address, and the router translates with NAT. With **IPv6**, every device has its own globally valid address. There's nothing to redirect. Instead, the router's firewall blocks incoming connections, and the "forwarding" is an exception in that firewall.

That has two consequences:

- You have to create the IPv6 forwarding **in addition**. On many routers, it's a separate checkbox or section next to the IPv4 forwarding.
- The forwarding applies to a specific address of the server. If the prefix your provider assigns changes, the router has to update the rule. Good routers do this automatically if you create the forwarding for the device instead of a fixed address.

After setting it up, check that your server is really only reachable on 80 and 443 over IPv6. A common mistake is an IPv6 forwarding for "all ports" of the device.

## When port forwarding doesn't work

Port forwarding requires your connection to have **its own public IPv4 address**. On many cable and fiber connections, that's not the case: with DS-Lite or CGNAT, you share the IPv4 address with other customers, and requests from outside never even reach your router. [CGNAT & DS-Lite](/en/wiki/cgnat-ds-lite/) explains what that means.

Then you're left with IPv6 (only for visitors who have IPv6 themselves), a [tunnel](/en/wiki/tunnels/) or a small VPS as the entrance. [Access methods compared](/en/wiki/access-methods-compared/) gives an overview.

## In short

- Only forward 80 and 443, both to the reverse proxy.
- Never forward admin interfaces, SSH or databases; that's what a VPN is for.
- Forward IPv6 separately and check it carefully.
- Without a public IPv4, classic port forwarding doesn't work.
