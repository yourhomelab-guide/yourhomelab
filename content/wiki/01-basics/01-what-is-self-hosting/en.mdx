## The idea in one sentence

You probably use many services today that run on someone else's servers: photos in a cloud, passwords in an
online vault, files with a storage provider. With self-hosting, you install comparable software on a server of your
own. The data then stays with you, and you decide who gets access.

A **server** is nothing exotic. It simply means a computer that runs around the clock and offers services on the
network. That can be a frugal mini PC on a shelf, an old laptop or a rented virtual server in a data center.

Many people start with one service and quickly notice how much more is possible. This hobby is often called a
**homelab**: a small IT environment of your own at home for running, learning and trying things out. What that looks
like for others is shown under [Homelabs](/en/homelabs/).

## Docker, VMs or Proxmox?

When you look around, you'll come across different approaches. **Virtualization** is popular: a hypervisor like
[Proxmox VE](/en/wiki/proxmox-ve/) runs directly on the hardware and splits it into several **virtual
machines** (VMs). Each VM is a complete computer with its own operating system and a fixed share of memory. That's
handy for running different systems side by side or for trying things safely with snapshots. There are also NAS
systems like [TrueNAS and Unraid](/en/wiki/truenas-unraid/) that bundle storage and apps under one web
interface.

Containers take a lighter route. They share the host system's kernel and only bring the application with everything
it needs. A container starts in seconds and often gets by with a few megabytes of memory, while a VM carries a whole
operating system around. The difference in detail is explained in
[VMs or containers?](/en/wiki/vms-or-containers/).

<Callout title="This site relies on Docker">
All guides here build on a Docker-based homelab: a Linux server with Docker Compose on top. That's the easiest to
learn, convenient to maintain (one folder per service, updates with two commands), needs the fewest resources and
also runs on a small mini PC or VPS. Proxmox and friends are great, but add an extra layer you don't need at the
start. If you want to virtualize later, everything from here works the same in a Debian or Ubuntu VM on Proxmox.
Which combination fits when is shown in [Operating models at a glance](/en/wiki/operating-models/).
</Callout>

## What it looks like technically

A Docker homelab as this site describes it consists of a few building blocks:

1. **Hardware**: a computer that runs permanently. Which devices are suitable is in the
   [hardware overview](/en/wiki/hardware-overview/).
2. **Operating system**: usually a lean Linux like Debian or Ubuntu Server, without a graphical interface. You manage
   it via [SSH](/en/wiki/ssh-basics/) from your normal computer.
3. **Docker**: each service runs in its own **container**, an isolated environment with everything the software
   needs. It's described in a short file called `compose.yaml`. More in
   [What is Docker?](/en/wiki/what-is-docker/).
4. **Reverse proxy**: a service that accepts requests and passes them on to the right container. It also takes care
   of HTTPS certificates, so you can use addresses like `vault.__DOMAIN__` instead of an IP address and port. See
   [Reverse proxy explained](/en/wiki/reverse-proxy-explained/).
5. **Backups**: a copy of your data in another place. Without this point, self-hosting is a risk.

All services live in a shared folder, `__ROOT__` by default. Each service has a subfolder with `compose.yaml`, `.env`
and `data/`. That way you always know where everything is, and in the end you back up exactly one folder.

## Where the server is

Self-hosting doesn't necessarily mean the computer sits in your living room. There are three models: locally at
home, in the cloud on a rented server, or a mix of both. Which one suits you is covered in
[Cloud, hybrid or local?](/en/wiki/cloud-hybrid-local/).

What matters isn't the location but control: you choose the software, decide about updates and know where your data
is.

## What you should bring

You don't have to be a pro. It helps to have:

- **Curiosity and patience.** Not everything works on the first try. Reading error messages is part of it.
- **Some time.** Setting things up takes a few evenings. After that, a few minutes a week for updates and a quick
  look at the system are usually enough.
- **Terminal basics.** Copying commands, editing files, changing folders. You'll learn the rest along the way.
- **A sense of responsibility.** You're now your own IT department. If something breaks, there's no support line
  to call.

Whether the effort is worth it for you is looked at honestly, with pros and cons, in
[Is it worth it?](/en/wiki/is-it-worth-it/).
