## The short answer

Self-hosting is worth it if you enjoy tinkering, want to keep your data in your own hands and are willing to look
after your system regularly. It's less worth it if you only want to save money or if a service has to be reachable
under all circumstances.

## What speaks for it

- **Control over your data.** Photos, passwords and documents live on your hardware. No provider analyzes them,
  suddenly changes the terms or shuts the service down.
- **No subscription spiral.** Many cloud services cost money every month, per person or per gigabyte. A server of
  your own mainly costs power and the hardware once. How much exactly is shown in
  [Costs & power consumption](/en/wiki/costs-power/), and the
  [power calculator](/en/tools/power-calculator/) works it out for your device.
- **You learn a lot.** Linux, networks, DNS, certificates, containers: this knowledge also helps at work or in
  understanding how the internet works.
- **Freedom of choice.** You combine the software that suits you and aren't tied to one ecosystem.
- **Storage is cheap.** Large amounts of data like photo archives or media are often much cheaper on your own disks
  than in the cloud.

## What speaks against it

- **Time.** Setting things up takes a few evenings. After that, you need time for updates, occasional errors and new
  versions that do things a bit differently than the old ones. Expect about half an hour to an hour a week when
  everything runs, and more when it doesn't.
- **You are the support.** If a disk fails at night or a container stops starting, it's on you. If others (family,
  friends) use your services, they depend on you.
- **Availability.** Power outage, router restart, moving house, vacation: a server at home isn't as reliable as a
  data center. For most private services that's fine, but not for everything.
- **Security is up to you.** A service you put on the internet is found by automated scanners within hours. Updates
  and a small [attack surface](/en/wiki/attack-surface/) are a must.
- **Hardware and power cost money too.** Compared to a free offering, you don't save anything.

## The most important responsibility: backups

With a cloud provider, someone else makes sure your data survives a disk failure. With self-hosting, that's you.
It's the point beginners underestimate most often.

<Callout type="warning" title="No self-hosting without backups">
If you keep photos or passwords only on your server, without a copy in another place, they're less protected than
before in the cloud. Set up backups before you move important data, and test restoring at least once.
</Callout>

A proven rule of thumb is **3-2-1**: three copies of your data, on two different media, one of them off-site. What
that looks like in practice is explained in [Backup strategies](/en/wiki/backup-strategies/) and the guide
[Backups with borgmatic](/en/setup/backups-borgmatic/).

## When you'd better not

Not every service is equally suitable. Think carefully in these cases:

- **Mail server.** Technically possible, practically tedious. Many internet providers block outgoing port 25 on
  residential lines, and the dynamic IP addresses of residential lines are often on blocklists. To keep your mail
  out of spam folders, you need correct SPF, DKIM and DMARC records, a matching reverse DNS entry and a clean IP
  reputation, among other things. And if your server goes down, important mail may not arrive at all in the worst
  case. For email, a good provider with your own domain is usually the better choice.
- **Services that must always run.** If an outage causes real damage, for example with business data or services
  others rely on, you need more than a single server at home.
- **Only copy of irreplaceable data.** As long as there's no working backup, keep the originals where they are now
  as well.
- **If you don't have the time.** A server that gets no updates for months is a risk. Then a well-maintained cloud
  service is the safer choice.

## A good middle ground

You don't have to choose all or nothing. Many people run services at home where data ownership and storage space
matter, for example photos, files and passwords, and leave email with a provider. A rented server can also be the
right start, see [Cloud, hybrid or local?](/en/wiki/cloud-hybrid-local/).

Access on the go isn't all or nothing either: through a [VPN](/en/wiki/vpn/), you reach your services
without putting them on the public internet. That lowers the risk considerably.

<Callout title="An honest self-assessment">
Ask yourself: Do I enjoy setting things up? Do I have a little time every week? Am I willing to take backups
seriously? If you say "yes" three times, nothing stands in the way of getting started. Where best to begin is
worked out by the [getting-started assistant](/en/tools/getting-started/).
</Callout>
