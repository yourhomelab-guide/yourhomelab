## Why Single Sign-On?

With every new service, you have to create a new login. After a few months, you'll have ten user accounts and ten passwords
and ten places where you have to create an account for family members. Some services don't even have their own
Log in, and you're left unprotected.

**Single Sign-On (SSO)** consolidates this in one place. A central authentication service, the **Identity Provider (IdP)**,
knows all the users. The individual services now ask only him: "Who is that, and are they allowed in?"

Here's what you'll get:

- **One account per person** instead of one account per service and person.
- **Strong authentication through a single point of access**—for example, using Passkeys or two-factor authentication—even for services that don't support it themselves.
- **Protection for services that do not have their own login**, such as dashboards or admin interfaces.

## OAuth2 and OIDC: A General Overview

You don't need to know the protocols in detail, but the terms come up all the time.

- **OAuth 2.0** defines how an application gains access on behalf of a user. Originally, it was about
  Permissions ("This app can access your contacts"), not the question of who someone is.
- **OpenID Connect (OIDC)** builds on OAuth 2.0 and adds exactly that: an identity. After the
  Login: username, email address, and often groups as well.

The process is always similar:

1. You open a service, such as `photos.__DOMAIN__`.
2. The service redirects you to the identity provider, such as `id.__DOMAIN__`.
3. You sign up there.
4. The IdP sends you back to the service with a signed certificate. The service verifies it and grants you access.

To make this work, you register each service with the IdP as **a client**. You'll receive a **client ID** and a
**Client secret** and enter the **redirect URI** in the IdP—that is, the address to which it redirects after login.
The client secret is a password and belongs in the service's`.env` secrets; see [Manage Secrets](/en/wiki/managing-secrets/).

## Two Options: Native OIDC or Forward Auth

### Native OIDC

Many services support OIDC on their own, such as [Immich](/en/services/immich/) and [Forgejo](/en/services/forgejo/).
In its settings, enter the IdP's address, client ID, and client secret. The following appears on the login page:
then a button that says "Sign in with Pocket ID."

This is the cleanest approach: The service knows who is logged in, creates the appropriate user accounts, and can assign permissions
assigned. Apps on cell phones usually work without any problems, too, because the service manages the process itself.

### Forward Auth

Not every service supports OIDC. That's where **Forward Auth** comes in: the [reverse proxy](/en/wiki/reverse-proxy-explained/)
For every request, first check with an authentication service to see if the visitor is logged in. The proxy will only proceed if the visitor agrees.
forward the request to the actual service. Otherwise, the visitor is redirected to a login page.

In Traefik, this is a middleware component that you attach to a router using a label. Caddy uses Nginx for this`forward_auth`.
Proxy Manager can be integrated using its own Nginx configuration.

Forward Auth protects every service, but it has its limitations:

- The service itself isn't aware of this. If it has its own login, you'll have to log in twice.
- Mobile apps and other programs that communicate directly with the API can't do anything with the login page, and
  often fail. For such services, native OIDC is better.

| | Native OIDC | Forward Auth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The service has to be able to do it** | Yes | No |
| **User Accounts in the Service** | Are created automatically | Stay apart |
| **Apps and APIs** | Usually work | Often problematic |
| **Good for** | Services with OIDC Support | Dashboards, Admin Interfaces, Services That Don't Require a Login |

In practice, you combine both: native OIDC when the service supports it, and Forward Auth for everything else.

For most home labs, the combination of [Pocket ID](/en/services/pocket-id/) and [Tinyauth](/en/services/tinyauth/) is sufficient:
Pocket ID is the central login; Tinyauth protects services that do not use OIDC via forward authentication and uses Pocket ID for this purpose as

## What You Should Keep in Mind

- **The IdP is a key component.** If it goes down, you won't be able to access any connected services. Keep in
  Set up a local admin account as an emergency access point for critical services and back up the IdP's data.
- **HTTPS is required.** OIDC only works reliably with valid certificates; see
  [TLS & Certificates](/en/wiki/tls-certificates/).
- **Enter the redirect URIs exactly as shown.** A typo or  `http`instead of  is`https` the most common reason for
  Error messages upon first login.
