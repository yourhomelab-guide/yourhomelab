## Why single sign-on?

Every new service brings a new login. After a few months, you have ten user accounts, ten passwords and ten places
where you have to create an account for family members. Some services have no login of their own at all and stand
there unprotected.

**Single sign-on (SSO)** bundles this in one place. A central login service, the **identity provider (IdP)**, knows
all users. The individual services only ask it: "Who is this, and may they come in?"

That gets you:

- **One account per person** instead of one account per service and person.
- **Strong login in one place**, for example with passkeys or a second factor, even for services that can't do that
  themselves.
- **Protection for services without a login of their own**, for example dashboards or admin interfaces.

## OAuth2 and OIDC in broad strokes

You don't need to know the protocols in detail, but the terms come up everywhere.

- **OAuth 2.0** governs how an application gets access on behalf of a user. Originally it was about permissions
  ("This app may read your contacts"), not about who someone is.
- **OpenID Connect (OIDC)** builds on OAuth 2.0 and adds exactly that: an identity. After login, the service learns
  the user name, email address and often groups too.

The flow always looks similar:

1. You open a service, for example `photos.__DOMAIN__`.
2. The service redirects you to the identity provider, for example `id.__DOMAIN__`.
3. You log in there.
4. The IdP sends you back to the service with a signed proof. The service checks it and lets you in.

For this to work, you register each service with the IdP as a **client**. You get a **client ID** and a **client
secret**, and you enter the **redirect URI** at the IdP, the address it sends you back to after login. The client
secret is a password and belongs in the service's `.env`, see [Managing secrets](/en/wiki/managing-secrets/).

## Two ways: native OIDC or forward auth

### Native OIDC

Many services can do OIDC themselves, for example [Immich](/en/services/immich/) and [Forgejo](/en/services/forgejo/).
In their settings, you enter the IdP's address, client ID and client secret. A button like "Log in with Pocket ID"
then appears on the login page.

That's the cleanest way: the service knows who is logged in, creates matching user accounts and can assign
permissions. Phone apps usually work without problems too, because the service controls the flow itself.

### Forward auth

Not every service can do OIDC. Then **forward auth** helps: with every request, the
[reverse proxy](/en/wiki/reverse-proxy-explained/) first asks a login service whether the visitor is logged in.
Only if it agrees does the proxy pass the request on to the actual service. Otherwise the visitor lands on a login
page.

In Traefik, that's a middleware you attach to a router with a label. Caddy has `forward_auth` for this, and Nginx
Proxy Manager can be connected through custom Nginx configuration.

Forward auth protects any service, but it has limits:

- The service itself knows nothing about it. If it has a login of its own, you log in twice.
- Phone apps and other programs that talk directly to the API can't deal with the login page and often fail. For such
  services, native OIDC is better.

| | Native OIDC | Forward auth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Service must support it** | Yes | No |
| **User accounts in the service** | Created automatically | Stay separate |
| **Apps and API** | Usually work | Often problematic |
| **Good for** | Services with OIDC support | Dashboards, admin interfaces, services without a login |

In practice, you combine both: native OIDC where the service offers it, forward auth for the rest.

## Which tool?

<Cards>
  <Card title="Pocket ID">
    Lean OIDC provider. Login with passkeys only, no passwords. Little setup, few resources. Ideal for services with
    native OIDC support.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Tinyauth">
    Small login middleware for forward auth in front of Traefik, Caddy or NPM. Can manage users itself or pass the login
    on to Pocket ID. The [password hash](/en/tools/password-hash/) tool creates the password hash for local users.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Authentik">
    Full identity provider with OIDC, SAML, LDAP and its own proxy. Very powerful, but more complex and considerably
    hungrier at around 2 GB of RAM.
  </Card>
</Cards>

For most homelabs, the duo of [Pocket ID](/en/services/pocket-id/) and [Tinyauth](/en/services/tinyauth/) is enough:
Pocket ID is the central login, and Tinyauth protects the services without OIDC via forward auth, using Pocket ID for
the login. [Authentik](/en/services/authentik/) is worth it if you want to manage many users, groups and rules or need
protocols like LDAP.

## What to watch out for

- **The IdP is a master key.** If it fails, you can't get into any connected service. Keep a local admin account as
  emergency access in important services, and back up the IdP's data too.
- **HTTPS is mandatory.** OIDC only works reliably with valid certificates, see
  [TLS & certificates](/en/wiki/tls-certificates/).
- **Enter redirect URIs exactly.** A typo or `http` instead of `https` is the most common reason for error messages
  at the first login.
